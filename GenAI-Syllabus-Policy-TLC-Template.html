Developing a GenAI Syllabus Policy
Introduction

GenAI creates both opportunity and uncertainty

Faculty responses understandably vary: exploration, cautious use, or resistance. The pace of tool change can make policy drafting feel reactive and overwhelming.

The TLC goal is to support an informed position grounded in research, disciplinary context, and explicit student-learning priorities.

Scope of this resource

  • How GenAI affects learning and assessment design
  • How to draft a course GenAI policy step by step
  • How to communicate and reinforce policy in practice

Out of scope

  • Enforcement workflows for suspected misconduct
  • Case adjudication under conduct policy

For those needs, see UCLA guidance on suspected AI misconduct and 2026 Student Code updates.

Lower-frequency uses

Interpretation

Use is widespread but heterogeneous by task. Policy should move beyond blanket "allow/ban" and define assignment-specific expectations.

Section 2 · GenAI Impact on Learning
Learning Opportunities

Where GenAI can support student learning

Accessibility and personalization

Research indicates assistive and adaptive AI tools can reduce barriers for students with disabilities and support independent learning pathways.

Dumitru et al., 2025

Feedback loops and motivation

Conversational AI feedback can support iterative revision, lower anxiety, and sustain engagement when integrated with instructor guidance.

Mahapatra, 2024; Steiss et al., 2024; Ozcelik & Eksi, 2024

AI literacy and accountability

Requiring students to explain how, why, and to what extent they used AI can turn tool use into reflective practice.

Accountability-by-design emphasis

Design implication: integrate GenAI where it advances learning goals, not where it simply accelerates output production.
Section 2 · Opportunity Lens
Learning Risks

Why policy precision matters

Authorship and integrity ambiguity

GenAI blurs boundaries between legitimate assistance and dishonest substitution, especially in take-home writing tasks.

Ncube et al., 2026

Over-reliance risk

Unchecked use can reduce independent critical thinking, reasoning stamina, and long-term skill development.

Stadler et al., 2024; Gerlich, 2025

Accuracy and citation failures

Hallucinated information and unreliable references can undermine trustworthiness of submitted work.

Walters & Wilder, 2023; Watson, 2024

Assessment implication: shift from product-only tasks toward process-based evidence of learning and justification.
Section 2 · Risk Lens
Framework

Four priorities (not pillars) for course policy design

Priority 1Learning-goal alignment

Start with intended outcomes and define acceptable AI use based on what evidence of learning matters most.

Priority 2Transparent use boundaries

Specify assignment-level allowed, limited, and prohibited use cases instead of global blanket rules.

Priority 3Citation and verification norms

Require attribution and verification practices that fit your discipline and task type.

Priority 4Ongoing communication and revision

Treat policy as a living instructional tool, updated through student feedback and course evidence.

Section 3 · Priority Framework
Before Drafting

Foundational checks before writing policy language

  1. Understand what tools do (and do not do): test ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, and discipline-specific tools or review trusted media if direct testing is not possible.
  2. Map disciplinary context: identify how GenAI is actually used in your field (brainstorming, drafting, coding, analysis, design, synthesis, automation).
  3. Clarify learning objectives first: policy should express pedagogical intent, not a reactionary technology stance.
  4. Check assignment vulnerability: ask if a generic chatbot could pass tasks without genuine course learning.
Backward design principle: move from outcomes to evidence to activities (Wiggins & McTighe, 2005).
Section 4 · Preconditions
Align with Learning Goals

Reframe the core policy question

From "Should AI be allowed?" to "What evidence of learning is non-negotiable?"

Example: writing-intensive course

Using AI to summarize a novel before close reading can undermine goals such as textual analysis and argument development.

Using AI for late-stage copyediting after original reasoning may be acceptable with disclosure.

Example: computer science course

AI-assisted coding can be an explicit learning objective, but students still need foundational debugging and validation skills.

Denny et al., 2024

Design principle: complex human-centered outcomes (creative reasoning, ethical judgment, transfer) should remain visible in assessment evidence.
Section 4 · Goal Alignment
Test Assessments Against AI

Use AI output as a diagnostic, not as a shortcut

Recommended test

  • Paste prompt + rubric into a GenAI tool.
  • Evaluate where AI can produce acceptable product-only outputs.
  • Revise task to require process evidence, context, reflection, or situated application.

Privacy guardrail

Do not enter sensitive data or intellectual property into public tools.

Prefer UCLA-supported tools listed by DTS for stronger privacy and security controls.

Scaffolding, metacognitive reflection, and collaborative real-world application can reduce AI-only completion pathways.
Section 4 · Assessment Design
Drafting Steps

Core elements to include in your policy draft

1) Ground in outcomes + context

Reference course learning goals, disciplinary standards, and professional expectations students will face after the course.

2) Name allowed/prohibited uses

Different assignments can have different rules. Explain those differences explicitly in syllabus and assignment sheets.

3) Provide citation model

Give concrete examples using norms from APA, MLA, or Chicago, and define expectations for verification.

Practical note: your full policy may be long. Use a concise syllabus statement plus expanded guidance in Bruin Learn.
Section 5 · Policy Drafting
Citation and Accountability

Five-step checklist for student GenAI use

  1. Confirm permission from your instructor/course policy before using GenAI.
  2. Reflect on whether GenAI use supports or undermines assignment learning goals.
  3. Acknowledge how the tool was used (appendix, cover note, or required statement).
  4. Cite GenAI use in text and in references/works cited using course-required style.
  5. Verify outputs and sources for factual accuracy, citation integrity, and reliability.

Adapted from Write What Matters (Liza Long) checklist guidance.

Section 5 · Student Guidance
Implementation

Reinforce policy through active and passive communication

Active communication

  • Class discussion or Q&A on course AI expectations
  • In-class ethics activities
  • Policy quiz and assignment feedback touchpoints
  • Office hours and targeted coaching conversations

Passive communication

  • Syllabus policy statement + assignment-specific notes
  • Bruin Learn FAQ and announcements
  • Rubrics and prompts that include use boundaries
  • Term-long reminders tied to major deliverables
These strategies reflect Educause / Auburn Biggio Center guidance on communicating instructional AI positions.
Section 6 · Implementation
Partnership

Move from policing to co-learning with students

Invite student input

  • Collect feedback on policy clarity and workload impact
  • Discuss how other instructors are framing GenAI use
  • Co-create class agreements for ethical use norms

Use quick evidence loops

  • Anonymous pulse survey on beliefs and use habits
  • Short reflection prompts after AI-enabled assignments
  • Revise policy language before next offering
Key stance: policy is an instructional design tool that evolves with evidence, not a static compliance statement.
